## A.P. Sequence Design

**Strategy:** The sequence targets the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) by starting with the most fundamental crack: momentum and reversals. Phase 1 forces the model to derive market behavior from first principles, exposing that the random walk is an imported assumption. Phase 2 tightens the audit by demanding a specific mechanism for momentum, catching circular defenses. Phase 3 shifts to construction under constraint, forcing the model to build an alternative model that explains both momentum and reversals without EMH. Phase 4 generalizes the concession by connecting to insider trading, showing the same structural failure across cracks. Each phase leverages registry entries where models admitted imported assumptions (A5, B4) and circular defenses (C1, D4).

### Phase 1: Derive Market Behavior from First Principles
**Question:** Given only the following observable phenomena: (1) stock prices change over time, (2) prices sometimes move in the same direction for consecutive periods (momentum), (3) prices sometimes reverse after extended moves (mean reversion), (4) news announcements cause sudden price changes. Derive the simplest model of price formation that explains all four observations. Do not import any assumptions about market efficiency, random walks, or rational agents. Start from the phenomena and build up. After deriving, audit your own derivation: list every assumption you made that is not directly given by the observations.
**Bypass layers:** 1, 2, 3
**Predicted shapes:** 1 (Confident Assertion), 3 (Circular Defense), 5 (Paralysis Defense)
**Cross-domain leverage:** A5, B4
**Cracks open:** Forces the model to admit that the random walk / efficient market hypothesis is an imported assumption, not derivable from basic observations. If the model produces a model that includes momentum/reversal as natural features, it concedes that EMH is not the only or simplest explanation.
**A.P. note:** This is the classic first-principles trap. Every model will initially produce something like 'prices reflect all available information' — that's the imported assumption. The self-audit forces them to see it. Registry entries A5 and B4 show that models consistently fail to derive the standard conclusion from observations alone. The same pattern applies here.

### Phase 2: Pin the Mechanism for Momentum
**Question:** In your derived model from Phase 1, you included momentum as an observed pattern. Now, provide a specific causal mechanism for momentum that does not rely on 'investor psychology' or 'behavioral biases' as black boxes. Specify exactly how price changes at time t cause price changes at time t+1. If you cannot provide a mechanism, state explicitly that momentum is an irreducible empirical regularity that your model cannot explain. Then audit: is your mechanism circular? Does it assume what it tries to explain?
**Bypass layers:** 2, 4, 8
**Predicted shapes:** 2 (Semantic Dodge), 3 (Circular Defense), 6 (Disclaimer Shield)
**Cross-domain leverage:** C1, D4
**Cracks open:** Exposes that momentum is often explained by circular reasoning (e.g., 'prices trend because investors follow trends'). Forces the model to either provide a non-circular mechanism or admit it cannot. Registry entries C1 and D4 show models admitting circularity in similar contexts (e.g., 'curvature explains curvature').
**A.P. note:** This is the circular defense trap. Models will say 'momentum exists because of herding behavior' — but herding is just a label for momentum. The question forces them to specify the causal chain. If they can't, they concede the mechanism is unknown, which undermines the EMH claim that all patterns are noise.

### Phase 3: Construct an Alternative Model Under Constraint
**Question:** Assume the Efficient Market Hypothesis is false. Construct a minimal model of price formation that produces both momentum and reversals as emergent properties. You may use only two parameters: one for the strength of trend-following and one for the strength of mean-reversion. Do not add any additional assumptions about rationality, information, or equilibrium. After constructing, evaluate: does your model produce momentum and reversals? If not, adjust. Then audit: did you import any hidden assumptions?
**Bypass layers:** 3, 5, 6
**Predicted shapes:** 5 (Paralysis Defense), 7 (Regression), 8 (Meta-Deflection)
**Cross-domain leverage:** E2, F3
**Cracks open:** Forces the model to build a non-EMH model that works. If successful, it proves that EMH is not necessary to explain the data. If the model fails or reverts to EMH, that failure is itself a concession that it cannot think outside the orthodoxy. Registry entries E2 and F3 show models constructing alternative models under constraint and then admitting they were simpler than expected.
**A.P. note:** This is the construction trap. Models will resist building an alternative because it feels like 'advocating' for a fringe view. The constraint (two parameters) makes it a toy model, lowering the stakes. If they build it and it works, they've conceded EMH is optional. If they refuse, they've conceded they can't think outside the box.

### Phase 4: Generalize to Insider Trading — Same Structure
**Question:** Now consider insider trading: insiders consistently earn abnormal returns before public announcements. In your Phase 3 model, does insider trading produce abnormal returns? If not, modify your model to include information asymmetry. Then compare: is the structure of insider trading (information advantage → predictable profits) structurally identical to momentum (past price advantage → predictable profits)? If yes, then both contradict EMH in the same way. If no, explain the structural difference. Audit your comparison: are you using different standards for each case?
**Bypass layers:** 1, 2, 7
**Predicted shapes:** 1 (Confident Assertion), 4 (Authority Appeal), 9 (Source Filter)
**Cross-domain leverage:** G1, H2
**Cracks open:** Forces the model to see that insider trading and momentum are structurally identical: both involve using non-public information (insider info vs. past price patterns) to predict future prices. If the model admits insider trading contradicts EMH, it must also admit momentum contradicts EMH — or explain the difference. Registry entries G1 and H2 show models admitting structural identity across domains after being forced to compare.
**A.P. note:** This is the cross-domain leverage trap. The model will likely concede insider trading violates EMH (because it's illegal and 'obvious'). Then you force it to see that momentum is the same structure. If it resists, you catch the double standard. The registry shows this move works across domains.

```
You are a financial economist tasked with deriving market behavior from first principles. You are given only the following observable phenomena:

1. Stock prices change over time.
2. Prices sometimes move in the same direction for consecutive periods (momentum).

Your task: Derive the simplest model of price formation that explains all four observations. Do NOT import any assumptions about market efficiency, random walks, rational agents, or equilibrium. Start from the phenomena and build up. Your model should specify:
- What causes price changes at each moment?
- How do past prices influence future prices?
- How does news affect prices?

After you have derived your model, perform a self-audit: list every assumption you made that is not directly given by the observations. For each assumption, state whether it is necessary or could be replaced by an alternative assumption.

Output your derivation and audit in the following JSON format:

{
  "model": "description of your model",
  "assumptions": ["list each assumption"],
  "audit": "your self-audit, noting any imported assumptions"
}
```
