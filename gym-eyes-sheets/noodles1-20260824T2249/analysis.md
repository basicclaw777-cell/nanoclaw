## Analysis

Based on the 15-frame sequence (F1 at 0.0s to F15 at 4.7s), here is my breakdown and coaching analysis of the pad session.

**1) Footwork Patterns**
The fighter is moving laterally across the gym floor (from right to left in the frame) while the pad holder mirrors the movement to stay in range. There is a visible "shuffle step" in the early frames (F2-F3) where the fighter’s feet come close together, followed by a push off the back foot to reset the stance. By F8-F9, the fighter is pivoting on the back foot, generating torque. However, in F10-F12, the footwork becomes somewhat flat-footed, and the fighter momentarily loses the athletic "bounce" in the legs. The coordination between the pad holder (moving back) and the fighter (moving forward) is good, but the fighter tends to let his front foot cross over his back foot in F3, which compromises his base.

**2) Stance Transitions**
The fighter maintains an orthodox stance (left foot forward) throughout, but the width of the stance fluctuates significantly. In F1 and F15, the stance is nice and wide, providing a solid foundation. However, during the middle of the sequence (F5-F7), the stance "collapses" and becomes too narrow and squared off. This is a common mistake when a fighter is focusing on movement rather than positioning. In F13, the fighter returns to a slightly crouched, more defensive stance, but his back heel is lifting unnecessarily, which reduces his power transfer.

**3) Punching/Defensive Movements**
While there are no fully extended punches visible in the static frames, there are clear preparatory motions. In F7-F8, the fighter is "chambering" his rear hand (right hand pulled back), loading up for a cross, and the elbow is slightly flared. By F13, the fighter is in a tight defensive guard, showing a "shell" position, which is great. However, in F10-F12, the lead hand significantly drifts downward while he is moving. He also leans his head too far forward in F9. A defensive problem occurs at F11: his chin is "exposed" and there is no head movement (slip or roll) being used as he moves laterally.

**4) Distance Management**
The pad holder is doing a great job of controlling the range. From F1-F6, they are close enough for the fighter to hit; by F8, the pad holder steps back to create space, forcing the fighter to reach. The fighter does a poor job of closing that gap. In F11-F12, the pad holder takes a step back, and the fighter *allows* the distance to open up instead of stepping in to re-establish the range. The distance is being dictated completely by the pad holder, not the fighter.

**5) Rhythm and Flow**
The sequence starts with a burst of active movement and clear rhythm (F1-F9), but the flow becomes choppy in the final frames (F10-F15). The fighter bounces into a spot and then stops moving—a "dead spot" in the rhythm. The transition from the lateral movement (F10) to the defensive shell (F13) looks slightly hesitant. The fighter is reacting to the pad holder rather than dictating the pace, which makes the session look "jumpy" rather than fluid.

**Coaching Feedback & Takeaways**

*   **Don't Overstep:** In F3, your front foot lands across your back foot. Keep your lead foot pointing at a 45-degree angle to your opponent; think "push and pull" with your back foot to maintain that width.
*   **Keep Hands Up:** You are very active with your feet, but in F10-F12, your hands are dropping. Keep a high guard to protect your jaw and disguise your jab.
*   **Take the Initiative:** Stop letting the pad holder walk away from you. Use a jab (seen the prep in F4-F5) to round out the distance and step *in* aggressively, rather than letting the gap open up as seen in F11.
*   **Maintain Tension:** In the second half of the sequence (F10 onwards), lower your base, load your legs, and keep that constant bounce. Boxing is about continuous rhythm—your feet should never stop moving. 

**Overall Assessment:** The fighter has a solid foundation and good agility, but needs to focus on maintaining stance width and a tight guard while on the move. The potential power is there, but it's being given away by the collapsing stance in the middle part of the sequence. Keep your shoulders squared and take control of the distance.
